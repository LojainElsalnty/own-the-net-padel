Level 1 · Start here

Know nothing yet? This is everything before your first point.

1

Before anyone hits a ball, the serving team is already split — one back, one at net. The returning team starts fully back.

Padel is always doubles. The server stands near their own service line, back from the net, while the server's partner is already up at net, ready to pounce on anything short. The returning team does the opposite at first: both the returner and their partner stay back, behind the service line — rushing the net before you've even seen the serve is a quick way to get picked off. Once the return is safely away, that pair moves forward together to even things up.

Serving team Returning team
2

The serve has one job: start the point low and diagonal.

The server drops the ball and hits it below waist height, after it has bounced once off the ground — never overhand. It must travel diagonally, landing inside the service box on the opposite side, exactly like a tennis serve. Serves alternate sides every point: right side of the court, then left, then right again, switching back and forth for the whole game.

Diagonal, cross-court only
3

Three ways a serve turns into a free point for the other team.

Land outside the correct diagonal box, step on or past the service line while serving, or hit it overhand or above the waist — any of those is a fault. Clip the net and still land in the right box, and it's just a let: replay it, no penalty. Two faults in a row on the same point is a double fault, and the other team wins the point outright.

4

The back glass isn't out of bounds. It's extra time.

Once the ball bounces inside the lines on your side, it's allowed to carry on into the back glass (or a side wall) before you hit it — you can play it straight off that rebound, as long as it's only bounced once on the ground first. It only becomes "out" if it lands outside the lines on the floor, bounces twice before anyone hits it, or hits the back glass or fence on the full, without bouncing on the ground first.

5

Pick up a pen for one second — the scoring is pure tennis.

15, 30, 40, game. What happens at 40-40 depends on which of three official formats is in play, agreed before the match starts, not switched mid-game: classic advantage (win two points in a row), golden point (the very next point decides the game outright, no advantage), or star point (up to two advantages are played, and if it's still tied after that, one final point settles it). Pro tours have mostly used golden point since 2020, with star point now appearing too — recreational clubs run all three. Ask before you start; it's the biggest rules variation you'll run into on a random court.

6

Six games wins a set. Two sets wins a match.

First team to 6 games takes the set, winning by at least two (a tiebreak settles a 6-6 score). Matches are best of three sets. Serve rotates by game, not by point: each player serves an entire game in turn, then it passes to an opponent, all the way around the four players and back again.

Level 2 · Play smarter

You know the rules. Now the court-position battle that actually wins points.

7

In padel, the net isn't where you rush to finish a point. It's where you live.

A player at the net can volley, intercept, and angle the ball into space the back players can never reach in time — while a player stuck near the back glass is almost always defending, not attacking. The single biggest tactical idea in padel is this: whichever team controls the net controls the point. Nearly everything else is about getting there, or stopping your opponents from getting there.

8

If you're stuck at the back, don't force it — lob.

Hit the ball high and deep, over the head of the opponent standing at the net, aiming for their back glass. They have to turn and retreat to play it, which empties the net for a few seconds. Use exactly those seconds: run forward together with your partner and claim the net position they just vacated. The lob doesn't win the point by itself — it wins you the court position that usually does.

9

When you get lobbed, don't always smash. Sometimes just stay.

If a lob flies over you at the net, retreating for a full-power smash often drags you straight back into the defensive position you were trying to avoid. The safer reply is a bandeja — a controlled, flattened overhead hit from where you're standing, which keeps your net position intact. Save the real smash for when the lob is short, you're balanced, and the point is clearly there to be finished.

10

Here's what all of that looks like strung together.

Serve goes in low and wide. The returner sends it back deep to keep the server's team pinned. A rally develops — until one team gets stuck defending and throws up a lob to buy time. The other team doesn't smash blindly; they let it bounce, play a controlled bandeja, and hold their net position. Two shots later, a short ball arrives, and now it's a real smash — into open space, past both opponents. Game point. That's a padel point: not about hitting harder, but about who's standing where when it matters.

Level 3 · Train your mind

The rules and tactics are only half of it — the rest happens between your ears.

11

Because of the wall rules, a point can look finished and still not be.

The ball keeps living off the back glass, so a shot that looks like a clean winner can come straight back, and a ball you've already written off can still land in play. Treat every point as live until it's actually dead — and reset completely the second it ends, whether you just won it or lost it. Dwell on a missed smash and you'll miss the next one too; relax after winning a big point and you'll hand the next one away. The players who look calm aren't lucky. They're just already thinking about the next point instead of the last one.

A short history, in three facts

Why it's even called "padel"

The word is just the Spanish spelling of the English "paddle." Enrique Corcuera had come across paddle tennis, an unrelated, wall-free American backyard game from the 1920s, and borrowed its name for the very different, fully enclosed sport he'd just built — the two games share a name and almost nothing else.

The walls used to be brick

Corcuera's original court was boxed in with solid concrete, not glass. Transparent walls became the standard later — partly so spectators, and eventually TV cameras, could actually see the rallies happening inside the enclosure.

It's been chasing the Olympics

Padel has already featured at multi-sport events like the World Beach Games, and its international federation has lobbied for full Olympic inclusion for years. No confirmed Games slot yet — but it's one of the most closely watched bids in racket sports.

That's the whole game

Now go lose some points on purpose.

Every rule, every tactic, and that whole business about staying switched on between points — none of it actually clicks from reading. It clicks from standing at the net too early, getting lobbed, losing the point, and doing it again anyway until you stop. Go find a court.
